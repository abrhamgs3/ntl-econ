Abstract 
This study investigates how Ethiopia's ethnic federal system, established in 1995, has influenced regional economic inequality and resource allocation patterns. Using satellite-derived nighttime light (NTL) data as a proxy for subnational economic activity from 1992 to 2021, combined with information on political leaders' birthplaces, we provide systematic evidence of regional favoritism in resource distribution. Our analysis yields three key findings: First, NTL data strongly correlates with World Bank GDP estimates (R² = 0.98), validating its use for tracking regional economic dynamics. Second, while interregional inequality measured by the Gini coefficient declined significantly from 0.4 to less than 0.1 during the study period, this aggregate improvement masks persistent disparities. Third, we find robust evidence that leaders’ birth regions receive disproportionate economic benefits, exhibiting 32% higher NTL intensity during their tenure (p < 0.01), a result confirmed by placebo tests (p = 0.001). These findings suggest that Ethiopia's ethnic federalism, while successful in reducing overall spatial inequality, creates structural incentives for regional favoritism. We conclude with policy recommendations for reforming fiscal decentralization to mitigate these biases, including formula-based resource allocation and independent oversight mechanisms.

1. Introduction 
Ethiopia’s economic trajectory over the past three decades has been marked by both rapid growth and persistent challenges (Faguet, 2021; Wubneh, 2017). Since adopting ethnic federalism in 1995 which constitutionally grants regional1 states greater autonomy, the country has undergone significant economic, and political transformations, with decentralization policies contributing to remarkable macroeconomic progress (Faguet, 2021), including an average annual GDP growth rate exceeding 10% and a decline in national poverty rates from 39% in 2004 to 27% in 2015 2015 (Dorosh et al., 2018). However, despite these gains, public sentiment in 2018–2019 revealed widespread dissatisfaction, with many Ethiopians describing the preceding decades as “27 years of darkness” (BBC_News, 2020). This disconnect between aggregate economic data and lived experiences underscores critical unanswered questions about whether growth benefits have been evenly distributed across regions or skewed by political favoritism within Ethiopia’s federal system.
Ethiopia’s ethnic-based federalism, designed to promote self-determination and mitigate conflict, carries inherent risks of reinforcing regional disparities if political leaders prioritize their birth regions or ethnic groups  (Alesina et al., 2003; Alesina & La Ferrara, 2005; Alesina et al., 2016; Easterly & Levine, 1997). By tying political representation to ethnic identity, the system creates a structural tension where leaders may prioritize their birth regions to consolidate ethnic support, exacerbating regional inequalities. While cross-country studies demonstrate that ethnic inequality can fuel political instability and undermine public goods provision (Alesina et al., 2016; Huber & Suryanarayan, 2016; Lessmann & Seidel, 2017; Mveyange, 2015; Singhal et al., 2020), empirical evidence specific to Ethiopia remains limited due to the lack of reliable subnational economic data. Traditional metrics like regional GDP are often unavailable or inconsistently measured, leaving researchers unable to rigorously assess regional inequality or detect favoritism. This gap is particularly pressing given Ethiopia’s ethnic diversity and the potential for regional imbalances to exacerbate social tensions (Alesina et al., 2016; Baldwin & Huber, 2010). Without adequate data, policymakers lack the evidence needed to address inequities or evaluate the effectiveness of federalism in promoting inclusive development. 
To overcome these challenges, we use satellite-based nighttime light (NTL) data as an innovative proxy for subnational economic activity, a method increasingly validated in development economics for data-scarce contexts (Chen & Nordhaus, 2011; Henderson et al., 2012). By analyzing NTL intensity across Ethiopian regions from 1992 to 2021 - and linking it to leaders’ birthplaces - we provide the first systematic assessment of regional inequality and favoritism under Ethiopia’s federal system. The study addresses three core questions: First, how has regional inequality evolved, as measured by NTL-derived Gini coefficients? Second, is there evidence that political leaders’ birth regions received disproportionate economic benefits, reflected in higher NTL growth? Third, how reliable is NTL data as a proxy for regional economic activity compared to traditional measures like GDP?
This research makes significant contributions to both academia and policy. Empirically, it offers novel insights into the spatial distribution of Ethiopia’s economic growth, challenging narratives of uniform progress and revealing potential biases in resource allocation. Methodologically, it advances the use of NTL data in fragile states, demonstrating its utility for studying subnational disparities where traditional data are lacking. For policymakers, the findings highlight risks associated with ethnic federalism, particularly the unintended consequences of decentralization in multi-ethnic societies. By identifying regions lagging behind or benefiting from favoritism, the study provides a foundation for targeted interventions to promote equitable development.
The paper is structured as follows: the next section presents the background, and Section 2 details the data sources and methodology, including the application of NTL data to measure economic activity. Section 3 outlines the empirical approach, while Section 4 presents the results and discusses their implications. Finally, Section 5 concludes with policy recommendations and directions for future research.
1.1. Background
Many developing countries, particularly those war-torn countries such as Ethiopia have a notable lack of reliable censuses of socioeconomic indicators, and only rudimentary economic statistics are available (Chen & Nordhaus, 2011). This hinders the attempt to understand and evaluate economic growth and poverty levels in these countries, more so regional economic inequality dynamics. Nonetheless, accurate awareness of the economic condition is essential for devising effective policies and economic participation. Previous studies on the Ethiopian economy heavily rely on the World Bank's data. It is essential, however, to note that it provides only various indices of the national economy and lacks data on nations, nationalities, and regional states.  
Ethiopia is a country of many ethnic groups, and ethnic diversity has both costs and benefits to the economy (Alesina et al., 2003; Alesina & La Ferrara, 2005; Alesina et al., 2016; Easterly & Levine, 1997). Income disparities along ethnic lines are likely to rise to political inequality based on ethnic identification and increase between-group inequality. According to Huber and Suryanarayan (2016), party ethnification in India is more pronounced in states with a significant degree of inequality across subcastes. Baldwin and Huber (2010) provide empirical data for 46 democracies linking between-group inequality to underprovision of public goods. Alesina et al. (2016) found a strong negative relationship between ethnic disparity and public goods in 18 Sub-Saharan African nations, which they attribute to political inequality and ethnic-based discrimination. Ethnic inequality can also impair institutional development and democratic consolidation (Robinson, 2003). In line with this conjecture, Kyriacou (2013) exploits survey data from 29 developing nations and found socioeconomic and ethnic group inequalities affect government quality. Given the theoretical difficulties and data challenges, detecting an empirically robust relationship between regional inequality and development in Ethiopia remains difficult for surveys.
Satellite data for evaluation of the regional economic inequality is more objective and dependable than the evaluations that mostly depend on expert opinions, selective surveys, and government declarations. The use of satellite data also provides a fresh look at countries’ economic performance by allowing for regional income dynamics and inequalities between regions. To establish consistent and credible regional economic inequality measures, we disaggregated the nighttime lighting at the regional state levels, applied the Gini coefficient index to measure the regional economic disparity over the study period, and examined factors affecting economic growth. Regional inequality can be caused by different factors such as preferential treatment (favoritism), differences in socioeconomic variables and institutional quality (Singhal et al., 2020). Our analysis also examined whether economic favoritism for the leader’s birthplace region, the leader region, occurred in Ethiopia.
To overcome the sparsity of regional income data along the regional state administration boundaries and to construct regional inequality measures for the states, we combined the latest Ethiopian administration boundary shapefile maps with satellite images of night light density, which are available at a fine grid. Recent studies (Bickenbach et al., 2016; Bruederle & Hodler, 2018; Henderson et al., 2018) have shown that luminosity is a strong proxy of development. This enables us to estimate regional inequality over time and evaluate it using Kuznet’s hypothesis. The nature of Ethiopia’s Gini Kuznets’ inverted-U hypothesis could likely be influenced due to federal redistribution policies targeting lagging regions.
In Ethiopia’s context, ethnic federalism’s dual mandate - to empower regions while maintaining national unity - risks being undermined when leaders exploit regional autonomy for patronage. The constitutional emphasis on ethno-territorial sovereignty (Article 39) may unintentionally legitimize favoritism by framing resource allocation as a matter of ethnic self-determination rather than equitable development.
This study is the first to examine Ethiopia’s regional states’ economic inequality trends and assesses whether economic favoritism for the leader’s birthplace region occurred over the last three decades. This paper puts forward and tests an alternative conjecture that posits the growth of the Ethiopian economy is not associated with a changing economic inequality among the regional states. Our thesis posits that the Ethiopian economy has been progressing starting from 1992 following the governance change, the distribution of economic activities among the regional states has been growing, but the leader’s birthplace region could benefit from disproportional growth.
2. Data
In our study, we utilize three datasets: (1) harmonized defense meteorological satellite program (DMSP) and visible infrared imaging radiometer suite (VIIRS) nighttime satellite images of 1992-2021 from (Li et al., 2023) and Elvidge et al. (2021), (2) world bank real GDP data, and (3) administrative boundaries for Ethiopian regional states. To work with these datasets, we begin by defining the period of analysis and setting up the directory structure of our computational environment. The start and end years are specified as global variables, which limit the study to a specific time frame and enable us to focus on a period of interest. The nighttime light data processed by Elvidge et al. (2021) covers only 2012-2021. In contrast, our study period covers two decades before that. 
 Real GDP data from the World Bank and NTL datasets from satellite images are used to assess the nature of the economic growth of Ethiopia and the regional state administrations in the country from 1992-2021. The administration boundary shapefile of regional states is used to construct the NTL luminosity of the regional states used to proxy regional economic development. Then, land area, regional dummy, and year are used to explain NTL luminosity and the extent of growth disparities among the regional states in the country. 
2.1. Leader Regions
This study defines the ‘leader region’ as the specific administrative region where Ethiopia’s sitting prime minister was born: Tigray for Meles Zenawi, SNNP for Hailemariam Desalegn, Oromia for Abiy Ahmed). This precise operationalization distinguishes it from broader power centers and allows for clear testing of birthplace favoritism effects. SNNP - at the time included both what is now Sidama and Southwestern Ethiopia. To assess this pattern empirically, our focus is on regions that have been or are currently leading regions within the periods. If these regions show no discernible difference during their leadership years compared to non-leadership years, it suggests that the increased NTL in leader regions may stem more from the political leaders themselves rather than from other regional characteristics. 
Regional changes in economic activity and relative importance typically evolve gradually, contrasting with the more abrupt changes in political leadership following elections or the natural conclusion of political tenures. Consequently, regions are anticipated to exhibit heightened levels of NTL before assuming leadership and potentially afterward, if changes in economic activity and relative importance are the driving factors behind the increased NTL in leader regions.
2.2. Real GDP
We use Ethiopia’s real GDP data from the WB converted at PPPs in 2015 US dollars. This data is used to evaluate the validity of the NTL data in approximating the economic growth of the country. The availability of regional NTL data allows for assessing the economic growth and economic disparities among the regional states in the country. In a related study, Feenstra et al. (2015) argued that the use of expenditure-side real GDP from Penn World Table 9 to compare living standards across countries and years is better. However, such kind of data in which real GDP for sub-national regional administrative boundaries is cross-sectionally comparable in multiple years is not available from World Development Indicators or Penn World Table. The alternative is to use NTL data and assess the economic growth and disparities among the regional states cross-sectionally and over the years. The variation in NTL is a viable instrument for cross-sectional comparability not only between countries but also within a country and each year (Pinkovskiy & Sala-i-Martin, 2016).  
2.3. Nighttime Light Data and Integration
Researchers are increasingly using satellite images of the Earth at night to monitor local economic activity, especially in areas where conventional data, such as GDP, is unavailable or where official estimates lack reliability (Patnaik & Mendez, 2024). Since 2010, over two hundred economic studies have been published using night lights data (Addison & Stewart, 2015; Bickenbach et al., 2016; Bluhm & McCord, 2022; Bruederle & Hodler, 2018; Fox & Verrucci, 2017; Kim, 2022; Mveyange, 2015; Singhal et al., 2020). Studies in other disciplines also use these data (Kondmann et al., 2020; Kondmann et al., 2021).  
We use two consistently processed annual global NTL time series from two sources: DMSP-OLS NTL Time Series, Version 4 for the years 1992-2013, and VIIRS for 2013-2021. NTL data is commonly used to represent the extent and intensity of human activities, including defining urban boundaries, mapping population density, and assessing economic development levels. Despite DMSP-OLS and NPP-VIIRS datasets being widely used global sources for NTL data, significant inconsistencies between them limit their effectiveness for long-term studies. With DMSP, annual data is available for pixels smaller than 1 square kilometer, rated on a scale of 0 to 63, where higher values indicate more intense nighttime illumination.
To create long time-series NTL data, DMSP-OLS and NPP-VIIRS data are often integrated with a focus on ensuring temporal and spatial consistency (Elvidge et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023). However, quality issues with these datasets remain significant, and a range of methods have been proposed to address these inconsistencies. The methods include resampling due to differences in spatial resolution, 30 arcsec2 for DMSP-OLS and 15 arcsec for NPP-VIIRS. Another major challenge is the difference in dynamic range, with DMSP-OLS using a 6-bit scale and NPP-VIIRS using a 14-bit scale (Li et al., 2023). 
The NTL data is constructed using inter-calibration methods for DMSP-OLS and NPP-VIIRS to create a pixel-corrected nighttime light dataset, which addresses pixel-scale research needs using the Consistent and Corrected Nighttime Light dataset (Zhao et al., 2022). By applying steps such as outlier removal, resampling, spatial masking, regression, and numerical calibration, the resulting dataset provides excellent temporal and spatial consistency, meeting pixel-scale research needs. 
Figure 1: Nighttime light intensity in Ethiopia (1992 vs. 2021). Brighter areas indicate higher economic activity. Addis Ababa (center) shows the most growth, while rural regions (e.g., Gambella) remain dim.
 
Without correction of the inconsistencies, DMSP data greatly understates spatial inequality, especially in densely populated places. Comparisons showing the superior performance of VIIRS data were not possible until recently when VIIRS data was converted into annual estimates for a like-for-like comparison with DMSP data. These findings suggest that researchers and policies based on their research should rely on the more accurate VIIRS data because the errors in DMSP data are likely to distort our understanding of on-the-ground economic changes (Gibson et al., 2024). 
Therefore, we combined the two data sources to extract annual panel data of NTL intensity at the country level and across its 13 regional states for 1992–2021. We adopt Hodler and Raschky (2014) to aggregate the light data into the sub-national region level and take the average of all the cell-level values within each regional state territory as a measure of light intensity at the regional level. For validation against real GDP at the country level, Henderson et al. (2012)  and Storeygard (2016) estimate the elasticity of real GDP with respect to NTL after they control for country and year-fixed effects in their estimation, and showed to be around 0.3 across different subsamples of countries. In the estimation of elasticity, Storeygard (2016) takes into account the lack of comparability of the values of light intensity across years.
Our study combines DMSP-OLS (1992–2013) and VIIRS (2013–2021) datasets to construct a consistent NTL time series. To address inconsistencies in spatial resolution (30 vs. 15 arcsecs) and dynamic range (6-bit vs. 14-bit), we apply pixel-scale corrections from (Zhao et al., 2022), including outlier removal, resampling, and sigmoid3 regression to harmonize values across sensors. While NTL intensity strongly correlates with national GDP (R² = 0.98, Table 1), we acknowledge its limitations as a proxy: urban areas and formal economies are overrepresented due to reliance on electrification, while rural and informal activities (e.g., subsistence farming) may be underestimated. These biases necessitate caution when interpreting subnational inequality, particularly in agrarian regions.
2.4.  National and Regional administrative boundaries
To aggregate NTL intensity at the 30 arc-second cell level into the country and regional state administration level, we needed spatial data on country and regional state administration boundaries. We use the 2021 updated Ethiopia national administration boundaries map, and adopt the procedure by Alesina et al. (2013); Alesina et al. (2016), and Dreher and Lohmann (2015) among others to compute NTL for regional economic data over the years. The focus, therefore, is on administrative regions at the second subnational level. Our sample consists of all the 14 regional states in the country as of 2021. Addis Ababa city administration is used as a reference category to compare regional economic growth and inequality in Ethiopia. In some cases, we dropped Addis Ababa from the sample and used other regions as a reference category in regional growth and disparity comparisons because Addis Ababa is a completely urbanized region which makes it different compared to other regional states. Therefore, the units of observation are regional states up to 2021 and the NTL intensity panel data is available for all years during the study period of 1992-2021. The regional states differ in their area, population, geography, and climate.
3. Empirical approach
In our study, the dependent variable ?NTL?_it, ? represents the average NTL intensity of pixels in regional administration, i, in Ethiopia for the year, t. The distribution of this average intensity is right-skewed, with a significant concentration at lower levels. Following Henderson et al. (2012) and Michalopoulos and Papaioannou (2013, 2014), we logarithmically transform this variable, by adding 0.01 to accommodate observations with zero reported light. This adjustment helps maintain the interpretability of coefficients, given the limitations in satellite detection thresholds and data processing. We document a consistently strong correlation between NTL and GDP at a national level, as noted by Henderson et al. (2012) ( see Table 1), indicating that NTL is a good proxy for GDP. This allows us to use the regional NTL data to evaluate regional growth patterns and inequalities.

The study employed an ordinary least square (OLS) estimator on time series data from 1992 to 2021 to estimate Eq.1. The balanced time series ensures no compositional bias in the estimation of year-specific effects. We then validate the predicted values of real GDP growth against the actual real GDP growth rate of Ethiopia observed by the World Bank over three decades. 
The NTL intensity data disaggregated by the regional administration boundaries is used to evaluate the growth rates and growth inequality of regional states in Ethiopia over the period 1992 - 2021. The main purpose of this study is to assess whether there is regional economic inequality and favoritism for the leader region. Favoritism refers to a situation where the leader’s region is preferentially treated and gains a disproportionate advantage, hence higher NTL intensity compared to other regions in the country. Control variables such as land area, regional dummy, time-specific effect, and data sources are also included to assess their effect on the NTL variation among the regional states. Therefore, the study employed fixed effect (FE) and random effect (RE) panel data models, in addition to pooled regression to estimate parameter ? and examine the presence of favoritism. This helps to test the validity of the estimates and account for both region-specific time-invariant and time-varying factors that affect the NTL intensity and economic activities across regions. Eq.6 in the empirical model is used to evaluate whether regional states in Ethiopia have unequal economic growth reflected by the NTL intensity whereas Eq.2,  Eq.3 and Eq.4 are used to assess whether the political leader’s birth region matters in increasing NTL intensity in his region. We also tested the lagged values of ?Leader_region?_(i,t-1) and other explanatory variables including the area of the region state, year, data, and regional dummies. The reason lagged leader birth region was used is that delays are likely between the central government’s decision on the allocation of public funds and the arrival of these funds in the targeted (chosen) regions, and between the arrival of these funds in the targeted regions and the increase in observed NTL via investment (e.g., construction of houses or plants), private consumption (e.g., electronic devices), or public infrastructure (e.g., lamp posts). The standard errors on the level of political leaders are clustered, that is, a cluster including all regions - years in which the country has been governed by the same leader or the same set of leaders. For consistency reasons, the clusters are lagged by one period. The regional state dummy variable, ?_i, indicates the region-fixed effects to control for time-invariant regional characteristics, such as endowments and geographic location. The year dummy variable ?_t, controls, in the most flexible way, for shocks and changes that are common to all regions in the country. Other factors such as data sources are included to control for the changes in satellite technologies and their sensor settings. 
Therefore, the coefficient on ?Leader_region?_(i,t), is expected to be positive, indicating regions have more intense NTL when a leader comes from that region than at other times. The positive and significant coefficient is interpreted as evidence for regional state favoritism. This interpretation rests on the assumption that central government leaders are the reason regions have more intense NTL when a leader region takes the leadership role. There is a potential endogeneity issue as some regions may simply have more intense NTL and higher chances of being the leader region than others. The use of region-fixed effects already ensures that the coefficient on ?Leader_region?_(i,t), is unaffected by time-invariant differences across regions. However, it could be that regions that are more economically active over time get more intense NTL and become more likely to be the leader’s region. 
To establish causality, we implement rigorous placebo tests by randomly assigning 'leader region' status 1,000 times, demonstrating our results exceed 99.9% of simulated null effects. The Gini coefficient analysis further contextualizes inequality trends across Ethiopia's federal system. We also assessed false positives by randomly assigning leader status to regions with a 10% probability in each simulation, preserving the observed frequency of actual leader regions.

For these reasons, the use of NTL density overcomes the biased selection of regional states into the publicly available samples and, thus, may provide a more complete picture of the economic activities of the regional states.

4.4. Placebo Leader region test 
Our analysis reveals robust evidence of regional favoritism in Ethiopia's economic development patterns. Leaders’ birth regions exhibit 32% higher NTL intensity compared to non-leader regions (? = 1.32, p < 0.01), indicating disproportionate economic benefits. Placebo tests confirm this finding is not driven by chance: only 0.1% of 1,000 simulated random assignments produced effects of similar magnitude (p = 0.001) (see Figure 5A in the Annex). The results persist across model specifications (FE, RE, and pooled OLS) and align with Ethiopia's ethnic federalism structure, suggesting leaders preferentially allocate resources to their home regions.
4.5.  Regional economic inequality trends
Inter-regional inequality is widely recognized as a key factor contributing to socioeconomic instability, civil unrest, and political polarization (Ezcurra, 2019; Mendez & Santos?Marquez, 2021). NTL data, serving as an economic activity proxy, has become a valuable tool for examining regional inequality and its evolution over time (Lessmann & Seidel, 2017; Mendez & Santos?Marquez, 2021; Mveyange, 2018). In this section, we analyzed the evolution of regional inequality through NTL data from 1992 - 2021, employing an established inequality measure: the Gini index. To measure regional inequality annually, we define a function that calculates these indexes for each year in our dataset. Before applying this function, we create a string-type vector to set the analysis timeframe. 
The analysis reveals a sharp decline in regional inequality, with the Gini coefficient falling from 0.4 in 1992 to 0.12 by 2021 (Figure 3) even if regional NTL and national GDP have grown from 1992 to 2021. This substantial reduction - equivalent to a 70% decrease - suggests that Ethiopia’s economic growth under federalism has been spatially inclusive, contradicting public perceptions of entrenched disparities. However, the persistence of relative gaps (e.g., urban-rural divides) requires attention, as “low” inequality may mask localized deprivation. 
Figure 3: Gini coefficient dynamics of (ln) NTL for regional inequality:1992-2021 

The observed 70% decline in regional Gini coefficients (1992-2021) initially appears to contradict Kuznets’ inverted-U hypothesis, which predicts higher inequality during early development stages. However, Ethiopia’s unique federal redistribution mechanisms and rapid service sector growth may explain this divergence, suggesting that institutional design can mitigate typical development-inequality pathways. The simultaneous reduction in spatial inequality alongside economic growth presents an important exception to conventional development economics frameworks. However, it is essential to interpret the results with caution, as the data involve potential measurement errors. The 70% Gini decline may mask persistent rural-urban gaps, as NTL underestimates non-electrified activity (e.g., subsistence farming). In developing countries, regional GDP data may not be available and be affected by limited coverage, price misrepresentation, and political influences. Meanwhile, even high-quality NTL intensity data may suffer from short-term lights, background interference, and other inaccuracies. Awareness of these potential errors is vital for accurately assessing regional economic inequality.
5. Conclusion
The increasing availability of satellite-based NTL data offers a valuable tool for tracking economic activity, particularly in countries with limited access to reliable official statistics. There is a positive correlation between NTL intensity and GDP, at both national and subnational levels. Leveraging this data, we investigate regional inequality trends and assess whether economic activity distribution reflects regional favoritism.
The following are the paper’s key results. First, estimations based on NTL observed by satellite show that the Ethiopian economy has been growing since the mid-2000s. This contradicts widespread public chant in the 2017s and 2018s that ‘Ethiopians lived in darkness in the past 30 years’, which pointed to no real progress following Ethiopia's regime change in the 1990s. Second, not only has economic recovery occurred overall, but the speed of recovery has witnessed low disparity or variation among regional states until 2017. The disparities could be directly tied to favoritism and regional endowments. 
The findings of regional favoritism and declining but uneven inequality call for targeted institutional reforms. First, Ethiopia should implement formula-based fiscal transfers using objective criteria (e.g., poverty rates, infrastructure deficits) to replace discretionary allocations that enable favoritism, modeled after successful systems like India's Finance Commission. Second, independent audits of major public investments in leaders’ home regions could curb bias while rotating infrastructure projects to historically disadvantaged areas would promote spatial equity. To improve policymaking, developing subnational GDP accounts validated against NTL data would address current information gaps, enabling better-targeted interventions. These measures should be complemented by adjustments to ethnic federalism, such as adopting proportional representation in budget allocation to balance ethnic interests. Such reforms would use Ethiopia’s existing governance framework while addressing the core challenges revealed by our analysis - the tension between rapid national growth and uneven regional development under the current federal system. By institutionalizing transparency in resource allocation and strengthening subnational data systems, Ethiopia could transform its economic growth into more equitable development while maintaining political stability. Future studies could also combine NTL with household surveys to calibrate rural economic activity.
The use of satellite imagery for economic analysis comes with certain challenges. Measurement errors in NTL data can result from various factors, including temporary lights, sensor calibration issues, blurring, thresholding, and variations in satellite detection angles. The use of multiple inequality measures could help to ensure the accuracy of regional inequality analysis.
